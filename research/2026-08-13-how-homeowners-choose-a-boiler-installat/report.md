## 1. PATTERNS

Genuine cross-source patterns are thin because the corpus is mostly fiction. What recurs:

- **Trust-via-transparency framing** appears in the only real source (V1: "transparent pricing," contractor vetting) and is echoed as a *narrative* device in fiction (V4: "we prepare ingredients via live stream — complete transparency"; V5: naming every simplifying assumption). The pattern worth noting: **transparency is repeatedly used as the trust lever** — the thing that converts a sceptical buyer.
- **Proof-through-diagnosis**: The expert wins by *diagnosing* something the customer couldn't see themselves (V1 satisfaction data; V4 food-fraud exposure; V5 failure-report reading). Across sources, authority is demonstrated by detecting hidden defects, not by claiming credentials.
- **Premium justified by reliability language** (V1): Trane "engineering excellence," Carrier "proven reliability." Premium brands never compete on price — they compete on *longevity and consequence-of-failure*.

## 2. OUTLIERS

- **V1 (Napoleon)** breaks the "comfort" frame with survival framing: "heating isn't just about comfort, it's about survival." This is emotionally stronger than competitors' bland reliability language — and directly relevant to a London boiler business (see Recommendations).
- **V5** is the only source where the expert is *disbelieved and punished* for being right. Its transferable idea: prediction + later confirmation is the most powerful trust-builder that exists (footbridge corrosion predicted → confirmed at exact joints two weeks later). Whether it "works" as a business tactic: yes, if you can document a prediction and its confirmation.
